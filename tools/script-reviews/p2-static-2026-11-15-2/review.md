## Findings

- **1.1a** [L3, would confuse or mislead her] "First, one quick question from the electricity lessons. What is electric current?" — The first question assumes she has already studied current. A first-time learner cannot answer it from everyday life or from anything the lesson has said, so her first experience is guessing or failing.
- **1.1a** [F3, would confuse or mislead her] "Yes: current is the rate of flow of charge. This lesson is about charge that stays put, or jumps." — 'Charge' and 'rate of flow' are used in the buttons and in every reply before anything has said what charge is.
- **1.1a wrong answers** [new, minor] "Not quite: current is the rate of flow of charge, how much charge passes a point each second." — Both wrong answers (heat, stored charge) get the same reply. Neither says why her own choice was wrong, so the reply does not fit the answer it follows.
- **1.1** [F3, minor] "This lesson is about static electricity" — The lesson never says what 'static' means (charge that stays in one place). The nearest it comes is a passing remark in the right-answer reply to 1.1a, which she only sees if she gets that question right.
- **1.2** [F5, a teacher would correct it] "Each atom has a tiny centre, called the nucleus, which holds particles called protons." — Atoms, nucleus and protons are all introduced in one short message.
- **1.2b** [L1, a teacher would correct it] "Charge comes in two types, positive and negative. Protons carry positive charge. Around the nucleus are much lighter particles, electrons, which carry negative charge." — Positive charge, negative charge and electrons all arrive in one message, straight after nucleus and protons. That is too many new words at once for a first-time learner.
- **1.2b** [F3, a teacher would correct it] "Charge comes in two types, positive and negative." — The lesson says charge comes in two types without ever saying what charge is (a property of some particles that makes them push or pull each other). The central word of the lesson is never explained.
- **1.10** [F3, minor] "An acetate rod is rubbed with a cloth and becomes positively charged." — 'Acetate' appears with no explanation that it is a kind of plastic. She may not know the word.
- **2.2** [new, minor] "two polythene rods are both rubbed with the same cloth, so both are negatively charged" — The 'so' does not follow from anything she has been told. The lesson never said that polythene gains electrons from a cloth, so she cannot see why the rods are negative rather than positive.
- **2.3** [F5, minor] "Two objects with the same type of charge repel: they push each other apart. A push like that between charged objects is called repulsion." — Two new terms, 'repel' and 'repulsion', in one message.
- **2.4** [F5, minor] "attract: they pull together. A pull like that between charged objects is called attraction." — Two new terms, 'attract' and 'attraction', in one message.
- **2.5 right answer** [P16, a teacher would correct it] "The jumper has rubbed the same charge onto every hair." — This is loose physics. Charge is not 'rubbed onto' the hair: electrons move between jumper and hair, and the hair may well have lost electrons. It should say every hair gains or loses electrons in the same way and so ends up with the same type of charge.
- **2.6** [F3, minor] "attraction and repulsion between charged objects are non-contact forces" — 'Force' is used here for the first time. Until now the lesson has only said 'push or pull', and it never says that a force is a push or a pull.
- **2.7** [F8, a teacher would correct it] "Metal and her body are both conductors, so some charge spreads onto her, and her hair stands on end. Which is true?" — The question has just said that charge from a negative dome spreads onto her, so the right button only restates it. The right option is also the longest and carries the explanation.
- **2.7** [new, would confuse or mislead her] "A girl touches a negatively charged metal dome. Metal and her body are both conductors, so some charge spreads onto her" — Step 1.9b said charge on a conductor flows away through your hand into the ground, yet here the charge stays on her and nothing says she is standing on an insulator. Charging by touch is also not in the lesson's key points, and 'dome' is not explained.
- **2.7 don't know (first time)** [F8, minor] "Electrons are negative, and they spread from the dome onto her. Which button says she becomes negative?" — The hint hands over the answer and asks her to find the button with those words. It is not an easier question, just a reading task.
- **2.7 wrong answer (protons)** [new, minor] "Not quite: protons never move." — This is overstated. Earlier the lesson said protons cannot be rubbed off and stay inside the atoms; 'never move' is a different and stronger claim.
- **3.4** [new, minor] "showing the way a small positive charge would be pushed if you put it there" — 'A small positive charge' is now treated as an object, where until now the lesson has spoken of charged objects. 'Pushed' is also awkward, because near a negative sphere it is pulled. It should say 'a small positively charged object' and 'the direction of the force'.
- **3.6** [F8, minor] "Around the positive sphere they point outwards; around the negative one, inwards." — The message just before the question says the lines around the negative sphere point inwards. The question then asks which way the force acts near the negative sphere, so she can read the answer off.
- **3.7** [F1, minor] "so the field is strongest there, and they spread out further away, where it is weaker" — That the field is strongest near the object and weaker further away was already said in 3.2 (and for the force in 2.6b). It is said again here, in the 4.5 replies and in the quiz. No question checks the new idea, the spacing of the lines.
- **4.2** [P16, minor] "Walking across a carpet rubs electrons onto you, so you become charged." — The lesson's key point only says electrons are transferred. The script states a direction (onto you) that it has no grounds for and that depends on the materials.
- **4.7 (all of them / none)** [new, minor] "A good line would say: a very strong electric field near a charged object; and electrons jump through the air." — The model 'line' is two fragments joined by a semicolon, not a sentence that answers 'what causes a spark?'. The same stitched wording appears in 2.8 and 3.8.
- **4.14 right answer** [new, minor] "It is a non-contact force; the electric field does it." — 'The electric field does it' is vague. It should say that each object is in the other's electric field and so feels a force.
- **whole lesson** [P14, a teacher would correct it] "Electric field lines around a positive sphere and a negative sphere." — Across four key points there is one static picture and nothing for her to try: no rods to hang, no balloon to rub, no charge to move near a sphere. Key points 1, 2 and 4 have no picture at all, so the 'Predict' questions are never followed by seeing what happens (also M1).

## Overall

Not quite as it is. The physics is mostly sound and the hints after "don't know" do get easier, but the lesson opens with a question on electric current that a first-time learner cannot answer, and it never says what charge is before piling on nucleus, protons, electrons, positive and negative in two messages. The metal dome question (2.7) gives away its own answer and contradicts what 1.9b said about charge flowing away through her to the ground, and the 2.5 reply about the jumper is loose physics. I would fix those and add something for her to do or watch for the rods and balloons before letting her use it.
